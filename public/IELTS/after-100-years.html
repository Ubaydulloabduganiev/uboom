Mortality, Meaning and the Use of Time

Adapted into IELTS Academic style from “After 100 Years, We All Will Be Dead”, published by @articles_in_english.
A

The statement that almost everyone alive today will be gone within a century initially sounds bleak. Examined more carefully, however, it can clarify rather than diminish life. People devote enormous attention to embarrassment, rivalry, status and criticism, even though such concerns rarely possess lasting significance. Awareness of mortality rearranges priorities by forcing a difficult question: if time is limited, what deserves it? Philosophers and ordinary people have offered different answers, yet many agree that life gains meaning not in spite of its limits but partly because of them.

B

Human attention is naturally drawn towards immediate demands. Bills, deadlines and social obligations feel concrete, whereas the distant future remains abstract. History nevertheless demonstrates the impermanence of nearly everything. A century ago, other people occupied today's streets, schools and workplaces. Their ambitions and anxieties felt no less urgent than ours, but most of their names have disappeared from public memory. Impermanence is often treated as a loss because people desire stability. Yet temporary experiences can become valuable precisely because they cannot continue indefinitely.

C

The apparent length of a century is also misleading. Psychologists use the term temporal perception to describe the subjective experience of time, which often appears to accelerate with age. Years that once seemed extensive may later pass with disturbing speed. This observation is intended less as a threat than as practical information. Wealth, ability and opportunity are distributed unequally, but every person receives only a finite lifespan. The crucial difference is therefore not whether time is limited, but how that limitation is used.

D

Death does not erase every consequence of a life. Ideas, books, discoveries and acts of kindness can continue through the people they affect. This relationship between actions and later results may be understood as causality. Human lives generate chains of influence that extend beyond direct observation. A successful entrepreneur once attributed the direction of his life not to a famous business leader but to a teacher who had encouraged him during adolescence. The teacher probably regarded the conversation as ordinary; the student remembered it for decades. The episode shows that influence can be nonlinear: a small intervention may produce an outcome far greater than its apparent size. Lasting legacies often emerge from ordinary moments rather than grand gestures.

E

Mortality can also be psychologically liberating. Much hesitation originates in fear of judgement, failure or embarrassment. People avoid opportunities and remain within unsatisfying routines because change carries social risk. Viewed across several decades, however, many such fears lose their importance. Existentialist thinkers argued that mortality should promote action rather than paralysis. If existence contains no guaranteed, predetermined meaning, individuals carry greater responsibility for the choices through which they construct it. A limited life makes indefinite postponement increasingly difficult to defend.

F

Modern societies frequently confuse activity with purpose. Existing requires biological survival, but living in a fuller sense involves engagement, curiosity, development and connection. Research on well-being repeatedly suggests that fulfilment depends less on endless material accumulation than many people expect. Money and achievement matter, but humans adapt quickly to improved circumstances; new possessions become normal and are replaced by new desires. Relationships, meaningful experiences and a durable sense of purpose tend to resist this process more successfully.

G

A cemetery offers a severe but useful perspective. The names carved into old stones belonged to people whose triumphs and worries once felt permanent. Recognising their disappearance can encourage humility, not as self-contempt but as an accurate understanding of one's small place within a much larger history. It can also correct mistaken ideas about legacy. People who concentrate obsessively on being remembered may create little worth remembering. Meaningful legacies are more often a by-product of meaningful living. Psychologists describe the pursuit of aims extending beyond personal interest as self-transcendence, and such contribution frequently produces deeper fulfilment than self-promotion.

H

If human beings were immortal, delay would carry little cost. There would always be another year in which to repair a relationship, begin important work or pay attention to an ordinary afternoon. Mortality gives these moments urgency because scarcity creates value. The temporary nature of life is therefore not only a tragedy; it is also a source of appreciation, courage and purpose. The author ultimately presents gratitude, rather than fear or denial, as the most rational response to mortality. A century from now, today's population will largely be gone. Today, however, remains available, which is precisely why it matters.
